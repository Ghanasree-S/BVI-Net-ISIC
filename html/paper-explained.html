The setting

Segmentation accuracy has mostly been bought with size. That currency doesn't spend in a clinic.

Good segmentation models got too heavy to use

Segmentation means labelling every single pixel of an image as either “this is the thing” or “this is not the thing”. Feed in a dermoscopy photo of a mole; get back a black-and-white mask where white marks exactly the lesion and black marks healthy skin. That outline is what a clinician actually needs — it gives the lesion's size, its border shape, and how irregular that border is, which are three of the strongest signals for melanoma.

The field has chased accuracy on this task the obvious way: bigger models. U-Net, the classic, carries 13.4 million learned numbers. VM-UNet, a recent Mamba-based one, carries 44.3 million. Both work well. Both also assume you have a discrete GPU sitting under the desk.

Why size is a real problem here, not a vanity metric

Two separate pressures push in the same direction:

  • The hardware is small. The useful place for this model is inside a handheld dermoscope, a clinic laptop, or a phone in a rural screening camp — not a datacentre. A 44M-parameter network doesn't fit that budget in memory, in power draw, or in latency.
  • The data is small. ISIC2018, one of the largest public skin-lesion sets, has 2,594 annotated training images. Medical annotation needs an expert with a mouse, so datasets stay in the thousands, not the millions. A 44M-parameter model facing 2,594 examples has enough capacity to simply memorise them, and memorising is not learning — it looks brilliant on the training set and falls apart on a new clinic's camera.

So “make it smaller” isn't only about deployment. On datasets this size, a smaller model is often the better-generalising model.

The gap the paper aims at

Lightweight models already exist — MALUNet, EGE-UNet and UltraLight VM-UNet all sit under 0.2M parameters. But the paper argues each of them buys its small size by giving up one half of what segmentation needs:

  • Lightweight CNNs (MALUNet, EGE-UNet) are excellent at local detail — edges, texture, the exact pixel where lesion becomes skin. But a convolution only ever looks at a small neighbourhood, so they fuse global context poorly. They can trace a boundary beautifully and still trace the wrong object.
  • Mamba / state-space models (VM-UNet and relatives) are the opposite — they see the whole image cheaply and know roughly where the lesion is, but they smooth over fine local detail and lose small lesions entirely.

Nobody had both at an ultra-lightweight scale. BVI-Net's proposal is that you don't have to choose, because biology already didn't.

The borrowed idea

Your visual cortex runs two parallel pathways with different jobs. The paper turns that split into an architecture.

Your brain already solved this, twice over

When light hits your retina, the signal doesn't travel one road to one “seeing” region. After an early relay, it splits into two streams that run in parallel and specialise:

  • The dorsal stream — the upper route, often called the “where” pathway. It is fast and coarse. It answers questions like where is that, how big, is it moving. It does not care what colour or texture the thing is.
  • The ventral stream — the lower route, the “what” pathway. It is slow and detailed. It runs through visual areas V1 → V2 → V4 → IT, building up from tiny oriented edges to textures to whole recognised objects.

The crucial part — the part BVI-Net is named after — is that these two streams interact. The fast dorsal stream gets a rough answer first, and that rough answer steers where the slow ventral stream spends its detailed effort. You experience this constantly: you catch a blur of movement in your periphery (dorsal, fast, vague), your attention snaps there, and only then do you resolve what it actually is (ventral, slow, precise).

In one line

Two lookers. One is fast and blurry and finds the thing; one is slow and sharp and describes it. The fast one tells the sharp one where to look. That's the entire architecture.

Component one

Ventral stream. Gabor-shaped kernels, four orientations, weights shared across the whole layer.

The Local Pathway: convolution that starts out knowing about edges

A convolution kernel is a small grid of numbers — here 5×5 — that slides over the image. At every position it multiplies its 25 numbers against the 25 pixels underneath and adds them up. The result is one number saying “how strongly does this patch match my pattern”. Slide it everywhere and you get a map of where that pattern occurs.

Normally these 25 numbers start as random noise and the network spends thousands of training steps discovering, from scratch, that edges are useful. Notably, when you look at what a trained network's first layer actually learned, it is almost always a set of oriented edge detectors — and those look almost exactly like the receptive fields neuroscientists measured in cat and monkey V1 neurons in the 1980s, which are well described by Gabor functions (a striped wave fading out under a Gaussian blob).

BVI-Net's move is to skip the rediscovery. Initialise the kernels as Gabor functions at four angles — 0°, 45°, 90°, 135° — so the layer begins where a trained network would have ended up, then let training fine-tune from there. Fewer wasted steps, and far fewer parameters needed to get there.

Orientation bias: V1/V2 and V4 are told to care about different angles

Real visual cortex is not neutral about angle. Neurons are measurably over-represented for some orientations, and the bias differs by area. The paper models two of these biases (its Equations 1–4) and hands them to different depths of the encoder:

Encoder stagesSimulatesOrientation bias applied
1 – 2 (shallow)V1 / V2Combined cardinal + radial — all four angles boosted equally, ×1.18
3 – 5 (deep)V4Cardinal only — 0° and 90° boosted ×1.26, the diagonals damped ×0.74

Read plainly: early layers stay open-minded about angle because they are still gathering raw edge evidence. Deeper layers are nudged toward vertical and horizontal structure, mirroring the cardinal-orientation preference seen in higher visual areas. These multipliers are fixed constants — they cost zero parameters.

The weight-sharing trick that makes it ultra-light

This is the part that actually buys the 0.026M budget, and it is easy to skim past. The paper states that the weights of the different orientation kernels in the same layer are shared.

Concretely: an ordinary convolution layer with 16 input channels and 16 output channels and a 5×5 kernel stores a separate 5×5 grid for every input–output channel pair — that's 16 × 16 × 25 = 6,400 numbers in one layer. The shared version instead stores one 5×5 grid per orientation — four grids, 100 numbers total — applies all four to every channel, and then uses a cheap 1×1 convolution to mix the results down to the output width.

Why sharing works here

An edge at 45° is an edge at 45° whether you find it in the red channel, the blue channel, or channel 11 of some deep feature map. There is no reason to learn that same detector 256 separate times. Sharing one detector across all channels is not a compromise — for oriented edge detection it is arguably the more correct design, and it happens to cost dramatically less.

This distinction — sharing the weights versus merely initialising every weight from the same Gabor shape — turned out to be the single most important sentence in the paper for our replication. Getting it wrong left our model roughly twice as large as it should have been. That story is in the companion document.

Component two

Dorsal stream. A state-space model reading the image as a sequence, four different ways at once.

The Global Pathway: FA-VSSM

The name unpacks as Fast-Attention Visual State Space Module. It has two channels working side by side: a state-space channel that reads the whole image cheaply, and a fast-attention channel that decides which parts of that reading to trust.

What a state space model is, without the maths

Think of reading a long sentence. You don't re-read every previous word at each new word — you carry a compressed running summary in your head and update it as you go. A state space model (SSM) does exactly that with numbers: it walks along a sequence keeping a fixed-size “state” vector, updating it at each step.

The reason this matters is cost. Transformer self-attention compares every position against every other position, so doubling the sequence quadruples the work — for a 256×256 image that's 65,536 positions and the comparison table is astronomically large. An SSM's cost grows linearly with sequence length. Mamba is the specific modern SSM the paper uses; its contribution is making the state update selective, so the model can choose to remember or forget based on what it is currently looking at.

The awkward part: an image is not a sequence

To feed a picture to a sequence model you must flatten it into a line of pixels. And the order you pick changes what counts as “nearby”. Read row by row and two pixels vertically adjacent end up 256 steps apart in the sequence — the model has to carry information about one across 255 unrelated pixels to relate them.

BVI-Net's answer is to not pick one order. It scans the feature map four ways, runs the SSM along each, un-scrambles each result back into image shape, and averages them. Whatever one scan order distorts, another preserves.

The “fast attention” half

Running alongside the scans is a deliberately cheap attention mechanism (the paper's Equation 5). It works in three steps:

  1. Squash the channels two ways. At every pixel position, take the average across all channels, and separately the maximum. This collapses a feature map of any depth into just two single-channel maps. Average says “how much is generally happening here”; maximum says “what is the strongest single response here”.
  2. One convolution. Slide a single 7×7 kernel over those two stacked maps to produce one map.
  3. Squash to 0–1. A sigmoid turns that map into a per-pixel weight between 0 and 1 — an attention mask.

That entire attention module is one small convolution. It costs essentially nothing, which is the whole point of calling it fast attention. The module's final output is the input plus the scanned global features multiplied by this mask: out = norm(x + attention × ssm_output). Global context gets added back, but only where the attention map says it is worth adding.

Component three

Skip connections that reason about regions instead of copying pixels.

GCN Attention skips: letting distant regions talk

Segmentation networks are shaped like a U. The left arm (the encoder) repeatedly shrinks the image while growing the number of feature channels — going from “256×256 pixels, 3 colours” toward “16×16 positions, many abstract features”. The right arm (the decoder) reverses that, blowing the small map back up to full resolution to produce the output mask.

The problem with shrinking is that fine detail is destroyed and cannot be invented back. So U-shaped networks add skip connections: a copy of each encoder stage's high-resolution features is handed sideways to the matching decoder stage, so the decoder gets both the semantic understanding from below and the crisp detail from the side. This is why U-Nets produce sharp boundaries at all.

But a plain skip copies everything at that resolution — the lesion boundary and also every hair, air bubble, ruler mark and colour patch in the frame. BVI-Net's skips do something smarter before handing the features over (Equations 6–8):

  1. Project pixels into regions. A 1×1 convolution plus a softmax assigns every pixel a soft membership across N graph nodes. Each node ends up as a weighted average of the pixels that belong to it — think “this node is the dark centre of the lesion”, “this node is the fading border”, “this node is background skin”. Thousands of pixels become a handful of regions.
  2. Let the regions talk. A graph convolution lets every node exchange information with every other node through a learned adjacency matrix. Because they are nodes and not pixels, distance on the image no longer matters — the top-left corner of the lesion can directly influence the bottom-right in a single step.
  3. Paint the answer back onto pixels. The same soft membership map, used in reverse, spreads the refined node features back over the image, and the result is added to the original features as a residual.
Why bother

A lesion is one object even when it has a pale centre and a dark rim that look nothing alike pixel-by-pixel. Reasoning over a handful of regions instead of thousands of pixels is what lets the model conclude “these two very different-looking patches are the same lesion” — cheaply, since the graph is tiny.

Assembly

Five encoder stages down, five decoder stages up, with the three components in their places.

The whole network, end to end

Each of the five encoder stages runs both pathways in parallel on the same input, gates one with the other, saves the result as a skip, then halves the resolution and moves down. The decoder walks back up, and at each level pulls in the matching skip after it has passed through GCN attention.

Training & measurement

Two losses because one is not enough, and six metrics because each hides a different failure.

How it is trained, and how it is scored

The loss: BCE + Dice, weighted equally

A loss function is the single number the network tries to push down. BVI-Net adds two together in equal measure (Equation 9), and the reason is worth understanding because it is a classic medical-imaging trap.

  • Binary cross-entropy (BCE) scores each pixel independently: how confident and how correct was the prediction at this pixel? It gives clean, well-behaved gradients. Its weakness is class imbalance — in a typical dermoscopy image the lesion covers maybe 15% of the frame, so a model that predicts “background everywhere” already scores 85% and BCE is only mildly annoyed. That's a very comfortable local minimum to get stuck in.
  • Dice loss scores the overlap between the predicted region and the true region as a whole. Predicting all-background gives a Dice of zero — total failure, no partial credit. This drags the model out of the trap. Its weakness is instability early in training when predictions are near-random.

Together, BCE keeps training stable and Dice keeps it honest about the shape.

The six metrics, and what each one hides

Every metric can be gamed by a differently-broken model. Reading them as a set is the only safe way.

Dice coefficient
Twice the overlap area, divided by the sum of both areas. The headline number for segmentation. 1.0 is a perfect match, 0 is no overlap at all. Rewards getting the region right as a whole rather than pixel by pixel.
mIoU (mean Intersection over Union)
Overlap divided by combined area. Measures the same thing as Dice but punishes errors harder, so it always reads lower. Dice ≈ 0.86 corresponds to mIoU ≈ 0.79.
Accuracy
Fraction of all pixels labelled correctly. The most misleading one here. Because most pixels are background, a model that predicts “no lesion anywhere” still scores in the high 80s. Never read it alone.
Sensitivity (recall)
Of the pixels that really are lesion, what fraction did the model find? Low sensitivity means missed lesion — clinically the dangerous failure, because it under-calls disease.
Specificity
Of the pixels that really are healthy skin, what fraction did the model correctly leave alone? Low specificity means over-calling — flagging healthy skin as lesion. Sensitivity and specificity trade off against each other, which is exactly why both are reported.
ASSD (average symmetric surface distance)
The only metric here measured in pixels, not percent, and the only one about boundary quality. It averages how far each point on the predicted outline sits from the true outline, and vice versa. Lower is better. Two masks can have near-identical Dice while one has a smooth accurate edge and the other a ragged one — ASSD is what separates them.

Put in context: 90.43 Dice is competitive with models several hundred times larger, at a size that fits comfortably in a microcontroller's memory and a speed that handles live video. If it holds, that is a genuinely useful result rather than an incremental one.

The catch

No source code was released with the paper. Every number above rests on a textual description of the architecture. Some details — the exact channel width of each of the five stages, the number of graph nodes N, the precise fusion arithmetic — are not fully pinned down in the text, and reasonable readings of the same sentences produce models of quite different sizes. Until someone rebuilds it from that description and measures what comes out, the 0.026M claim is unverified rather than wrong.

That is exactly what our group set out to do.

Jargon decoder

Terms in the order you are likely to meet them, not alphabetically.

Parameter
One number the network learns during training. “0.026M parameters” means 26,000 such numbers. It is the standard proxy for model size, memory footprint and overfitting risk.
GFLOPs
Billions of floating-point operations to process one image. Parameters measure how much the model stores; GFLOPs measure how much it does. A model can be small but slow, or large but fast, so both get reported.
Channel
One “layer” of a feature map. An RGB image has 3 channels; deep inside the network a stage might have 16, each holding a different learned property of every position. local global
Kernel
The small grid of weights a convolution slides over the image. A 5×5 kernel looks at 25 pixels at a time.
Gabor function
A striped wave fading out under a Gaussian bell. It is the standard mathematical model of what a V1 simple cell in visual cortex responds to, from Jones & Palmer's 1987 measurements — and, independently, what CNNs learn in their first layer anyway.
Weight sharing
Reusing the same learned numbers in many places instead of storing a separate copy per place. The single biggest lever on parameter count in this paper. local
Depthwise convolution
A convolution applied to each channel separately rather than mixing channels. Much cheaper than a normal convolution; usually paired with a 1×1 “pointwise” convolution that does the mixing afterwards.
State space model (SSM)
A sequence model that walks along the data carrying a fixed-size running summary. Cost grows linearly with length, unlike attention's quadratic growth. global
Mamba / selective scan
The specific modern SSM used here. “Selective” means the model decides what to keep and what to forget based on the current input rather than applying one fixed rule. Ships as a CUDA-compiled library, which matters a great deal in practice. global
Hilbert curve
A path that visits every cell of a grid such that every consecutive step lands on a touching cell — so flattening an image along it preserves spatial neighbourhood far better than reading row by row. global
Skip connection
A shortcut carrying high-resolution features from the encoder straight across to the decoder, bypassing the compressed middle. Without them, U-shaped networks produce blurry blobs instead of sharp boundaries.
Graph convolution (GCN)
Instead of neighbouring pixels, information is exchanged between nodes connected by a learned adjacency matrix — so relationships can jump across the image in one step regardless of distance.
Sigmoid
A function squashing any number into the range 0–1. Used twice here: to turn the Global Pathway's output into a gate, and to turn the final layer's output into per-pixel lesion probabilities.
Epoch
One complete pass of the training set through the network. Fifty epochs means the model saw every training image fifty times.
Ablation study
Removing one component at a time and remeasuring, to prove each part actually earns its place rather than riding along.
